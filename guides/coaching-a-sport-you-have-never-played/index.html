You've Been Asked to Coach a Sport You've Never Played

A PE teacher gets handed handball. A parent volunteers and ends up with the under-11 volleyball squad. It happens constantly, and almost every coaching resource online assumes you already know the sport. This one doesn't. Here's a first practice that works whatever you've been given.

The Problem Isn't What You Don't Know

The fear is always the same: they'll find out I don't know this sport. In practice, that's rarely what goes wrong. Beginner and youth groups are not lost because the coach couldn't explain a defensive rotation. They're lost because twenty players stood in one queue while four of them touched a ball.

At this level the job is organisation, safety and activity. A coach who has never played the sport but runs a session where every player is moving for fifty of the sixty minutes delivers a better practice than a former professional who lectures the group. That's the good news, and it's also the whole strategy: you can't out-knowledge the sport in a week, but you can out-organise it tonight.

Three Questions to Answer Before You Show Up

You don't need to learn the sport. You need three specific facts about it, and twenty minutes of reading gets you all three.

  1. What is the scoring action? Every sport has one thing that ends a possession in a point — a shot on goal, a spike, a winner down the line, a try in the corner. That action is your teaching focus for the first three or four weeks. Nothing else competes with it.
  2. What are the zones and where do players stand? Not tactics — geography. Which third is the attacking third, which zone is the setter's, which side of the court does the server stand on. This is what you'll draw, and it's what makes an instruction like "start here, run to there" possible.
  3. What is the one rule players break most? Travelling, double touch, foot fault, offside, carrying. Knowing the single most-broken rule means you can referee your own scrimmage without stopping it every ninety seconds, which is what actually kills a first practice.

Write those three answers down. That's your entire preparation on the sport itself. The rest of your prep goes into the session shape below.

The Four-Block First Practice

This structure holds in football, basketball, volleyball, handball, netball, hockey, tennis, padel, futsal, ultimate — anything with a playing surface and a scoring action. The blocks stay the same; only the content inside them changes with the sport.

Block 1 — Arrival activity (10 min)

Have something running before the first player arrives. As they turn up, they join in. Everyone has a ball, a racquet, a stick — whatever the sport uses — in their hands within thirty seconds of walking on.

Resist the instinct to gather everyone for an introduction speech. Your name, the rules and the season plan can wait for the first water break, when the group is warm and already treating you as the person running the session. Opening with a five-minute talk to a cold group of children is the most common first-practice mistake there is.

Block 2 — Teach one thing (10 min)

One skill. The scoring action from question one. Demonstrate it — or better, ask the player who clearly already knows the sport to demonstrate while you organise everyone else. Then everyone does it, repeatedly, in the simplest possible form.

This is where a diagram earns its keep. If you can't confidently demonstrate a technique, a drawn picture of where players stand, where they move and where the ball goes carries the explanation instead. Show the picture, walk it through once at half speed, start.

Block 3 — Small-group stations (20 min)

Split into two or three groups, each at its own station, each practising the same skill under a different constraint — static, then moving, then with light pressure. Stations exist for one reason: queues are the enemy. Three stations of six players beat one drill of eighteen every single time.

Rotate on a timer, not on your judgement of when a group is ready. A timer is fair, it's predictable, and it stops you having to make a coaching decision you're not yet equipped to make.

Block 4 — Small-sided game (20 min)

Play the actual sport, but smaller: fewer players per side, a reduced court, and only the rules you're confident refereeing. Four-a-side, three-a-side, two-a-side — whatever gets the most touches per player.

Finish here, always. Players remember the last twenty minutes of a session, and they should be playing the game they came to play, not being corrected. Let the scrimmage run over time if it's going well. Nobody has ever complained that practice ended with too much of the game.

Draw It Before You Get There

The single biggest gap between coaches who know a sport and coaches who don't is explanation. An experienced coach can improvise an instruction because they've seen the drill a hundred times. You haven't — so you plan the explanation instead of improvising it, and the fastest way to plan an explanation is to draw it.

Pick your sport from the sports hub, place the players, draw the movement arrows and mark the target zone. Four small diagrams — one per block — and your whole session fits on a phone screen you can hold on the sideline. When a player asks what they're supposed to do, you show them rather than describe it.

Two practical habits make this compound:

  • Name each diagram for the block it belongs to — "Week 1 — Stations — Passing", not "drill 3". Six weeks in you'll have a session library you can actually search.
  • Keep the ones that worked. A drill that landed with an under-11 group lands again next season. Redrawing it from memory a year later doesn't. See the play library guide for a naming system that scales.

Weeks Two to Six

Keep the four blocks identical. Players settle into a rhythm they recognise within two or three sessions, which means less time explaining the session and more time inside it. Change one variable per week:

  • Week 2: same skill, add movement to it.
  • Week 3: same skill, add a defender or light pressure.
  • Week 4: introduce the second skill — usually the one that sets up the scoring action.
  • Weeks 5-6: alternate the two, and lengthen the small-sided game.

By week six you'll have watched the sport being played in front of you for six hours, which teaches you more about it than any amount of reading. The knowledge gap closes on its own. The organisational habits are what you have to build deliberately, and you build them in the first session.

What Not to Do

  • Don't over-plan. New coaches routinely prepare twelve activities for a sixty-minute session and spend the hour explaining rather than coaching. Four blocks is enough. It is more than enough.
  • Don't pretend. "I don't know, I'll find out before next week" costs you nothing with a youth group. Bluffing a rule and being corrected by an eleven-year-old costs you a lot more.
  • Don't run drills you can't referee. If you're not sure whether something is a foul, design around it for now rather than stopping play every rally to work it out.
  • Don't skip the small-sided game because the skill work "wasn't finished". It's never finished. The game is why they came.

FAQ

Coaching a New Sport — Questions

I've never played this sport. Am I qualified to coach it?

At beginner and youth level, the job is organisation, safety and activity — not technical expertise. Learn the sport's scoring action, its zones and its most-broken rule, and that's enough for the first weeks. The rest you'll pick up by watching it played in front of you.

What should I do in the first ten minutes?

Have an activity already running when players arrive. Introductions and rules go in the first water break, when the group is warm and already listening to you.

How many drills should a first practice have?

Three or four. Over-planning is the classic new-coach error — twelve activities means you spend the hour explaining instead of coaching.

How do I explain a drill I've never run before?

Draw it. Positions, movement arrows, target zone. Show the picture, walk it once at half speed, start. This matters far more when you can't fall back on demonstrating the skill yourself.

What if a player knows more about the sport than I do?

Use them — have them demonstrate while you organise the group. The demonstration is better than yours and the player gets recognised. Pretending is the only version of this that goes badly.
