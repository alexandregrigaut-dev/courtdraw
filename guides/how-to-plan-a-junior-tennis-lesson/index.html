How to Plan a Junior Tennis Lesson That Actually Sticks

A junior lesson that works isn't improvised — it follows a structure players recognize, with a technical focus that carries through every block. Here's a four-part template you can reuse and adapt every week.

Why Structure Matters More for Juniors

Adult players tolerate a loosely structured hour. Juniors don't — attention drifts fast, and a lesson that jumps between unrelated activities feels chaotic even when every drill individually is good. A consistent four-block shape gives players a rhythm they recognize by week three, which means less time explaining what's happening and more time actually practicing.

The Four-Block Template

  1. Warm-up (5-10 min). Movement plus short-court rallying. The goal is raised heart rate and early contact feel, not technical correction yet.
  2. Technical block (15-20 min). Isolate one stroke or pattern — a serve toss, a split-step timing, a specific grip. Feed the ball yourself or with a machine so the player gets clean, repeatable reps at the target.
  3. Live-ball drill (15-20 min). Apply the technical focus under more realistic conditions — a cross-court consistency rally, an approach-and-volley sequence — where the ball is live rather than fed.
  4. Competitive game (10-15 min). A scored mini-game or point play that uses the day's focus. This is what players remember and what keeps them coming back — finish on competition, not correction.

Diagramming Each Block in Advance

Draw each block on the tennis tactics board before the lesson: the warm-up pattern, the technical feed with target zone marked, the live-ball drill positioning, and the competitive game's scoring zone or format. Save the set under the group's name (e.g. "U10 Tuesday — Week 4") so next season's version of the same lesson takes minutes to adapt rather than rebuild.

If you're planning a full season rather than one lesson, chain several of these four-block sessions together — see the Session Builder guide for how to sequence saved plays into a timed plan.

Rotating the Technical Focus

The structure stays the same every week, but the technical focus should rotate through your season plan — serve toss one week, split-step timing the next, backhand contact point after that. Keeping a saved library of technical-block diagrams by skill makes it easy to see at a glance what you've covered and what's due for a repeat.

FAQ

Junior Lesson Planning — Questions

How long should a junior tennis lesson be?

45-60 minutes works well for most junior groups. Younger or beginner groups (under 8) often hold focus better in 30-45 minute blocks with more variety.

Should every lesson follow the same structure?

The four-block shape works as a default, but the technical focus should rotate through your season plan so players aren't drilling the same stroke every week.

How do I keep a lesson plan for reuse next season?

Save each block's diagram in CourtDraw under a clear name. Reload and tweak it next season instead of rebuilding the plan from scratch.
